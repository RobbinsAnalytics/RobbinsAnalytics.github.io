0 · Premise

Terms used in this charter
Statutory clock
A deadline set by regulation; the workflow must protect it even when a feed fails.
Certified tier
The outputs that have passed the intended-use, review, approval and catalogue requirements stated in this charter. The label alone does not determine regulatory status.
Semantic model
A shared data model that defines relationships and business measures so reports can use the same definitions.
WIP
Work in progress: work already started and not yet completed.
Predicate rule
An underlying legal or regulatory requirement to maintain or submit a record. Quality determines its application to the output. FDA explanation.

Principle: principles are set in stone, methods are adjustable.

This charter is built that way. The principles are the rows above and they do not move. The practices under them are what I would do in a specific configuration, and they change when the evidence says so. Every practice carries a proof, a test and a mark, because I do not trust what I cannot test, and I do not present a plan as a record.

The configuration it assumes.

  • A data, analytics and automation team of about eight people inside a medical device quality organization: mostly analysts, one or two data engineers, data architects.
  • They exist already and are being brought together from separate units; they did not choose their manager.
  • They serve Regulatory Affairs, Quality Assurance and Clinical across several business units and sites, plus the quality-system process owners and IT.
  • The environment is regulated (FDA, ISO 13485) and fully remote.
  • The mandate is to unify the team, set standards and governance, recommend a structure, and take AI and automation beyond reporting.
  • The hardest part of the job is prioritizing competing asks and saying no.

This is a scenario. The team’s actual shape, systems, capacity and authority are unknown until discovery, and nothing below claims otherwise.

What that changes.

This is not a build from nothing. The first job is not trust in the data; it is trust in the manager, and then one definition of the work. Structure recommendations here are hypotheses with the evidence I would gather first, not a reorganization on day one. The sponsor knows the team better than I do.

Two rules of my own underneath everything.

A win for a person is a win for the whole team. Trust what you can test.

Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • This site records each module’s specification, evidence and review methods.
  • The Build by Build inventory shows which controls each build carries and the scope of their evidence.
  • The charter is held to the same rule: distinguish demonstrated practice, adaptation and proposal
How this would be checked
Every practice below has a proof row, a test row and a mark. Any that cannot be tested is labelled a hypothesis

1 · The 90-day plan and day-120 decision

Principle: change how we see before we change what we do.

The month-by-month.

Month one · Listen

  • Hold one-on-ones with each team member against six questions: competence, trustworthiness, energy, people skills, focus and judgment.
  • Interview each process owner.
  • Inventory current assets and definitions, open commitments, regulated-record outputs, the quality calendar, and service and support arrangements.

Month two · Prove

  • Publish the inventory and establish the partner forum.
  • Take one certified deliverable through the full accountability path, subject to access, controls and capacity.
  • Measure the baseline.

Month three · Recommend

  • Present a structure hypothesis supported by the intake log.
  • Show the options and what would change the answer.

Four milestones, kept apart.

  1. Day 30: a hypothesis to the sponsor, so nothing waits three months.
  2. Day 60: the operating arrangements in place (the board, the endorsement levels, the reviewer seats, the intake form, the forum); these are how the team works, not who reports to whom.
  3. Day 90: the structure recommendation with evidence.
  4. Day 120: the sponsor’s decision.

Reporting-line changes and people moves follow the decision, not the hypothesis.

What I refuse to change before the decision.

  • Reporting lines.
  • People.
  • Tools and platform.
  • Any feed that serves a statutory clock, until it is validated and has named coverage.
  • Inherited commitments, through their first full cycle.
  • One existing ritual that works.

Immediate safety or compliance action is never blocked by this list.

What changes in week one.

  • The intake form, as the inventory instrument.
  • The retrospective.
  • Weekly one-on-ones.
  • A written daily standup.
  • Named coverage for critical feeds.
  • One written definition of done.
  • The team page.

Forming the team.

A written transfer record from each former manager:

  • Recurring deliverables and their consumers; open commitments and dates.
  • Which duties transfer, which stay shared and until when; system ownership; one named tiebreaker.
  • Capability leadership moves to the new manager on day one: development, standards and promotion. Day-to-day priority remains shared until a written end date.

Two launch sessions in week one:

  1. The team reads and edits this charter live, writes its norms and names itself.
  2. The team meets without the manager to collect what it knows, wants to know and worries about. The manager returns and answers in the same facilitated session.

Capacity is a dependency, not an assumption.

  • Review, stewardship, delivery and support draw on the same eight people’s time.
  • Agree with the sponsor and team which existing duties stop or change to fund those responsibilities.
  • Set the allocation during discovery; none is assumed in this charter.

The first deliverable, a candidate.

  • Deliverable: within 60 days, a shared top-ten metric dictionary and one certified model on the highest-volume shared dataset, feeding the next management review.
  • Dictionary: owner, definition, source and version for the ten measures all three partners use.
  • Why this candidate: it involves most of the team across their former units, exercises every new mechanism once and uses a date the division already keeps.
  • Conditions: choose it with the sponsor, subject to access, data, applicable controls and capacity.
  • Fallback: publish the inventory as a catalogue if those conditions are not met.
Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Catalogued every pipeline in an operations analytics portfolio before cutting anything; retired dead pipelines and over-frequent refreshes for about $80K a year without moving or deleting a report anyone used.
  • Reconciling twelve KPIs across three plants took four months, not two; the inventory is what made the recommendation defensible.
  • The calendar and the transfer records are proposed
How this would be checked
  • Hypothesis delivered by day 30. Baseline scorecard at day 30.
  • Operating arrangements in place by day 60. Recommendation with evidence by day 90.
  • Nothing on the refuse list moved before the decision. Transfer records signed with end dates by day 30

2 · Team design

Principle: the person who builds a layer is never the person who verifies it.

The shape, as a starting hypothesis.

  • Hub: the manager, architect, one or two engineers and a senior analyst own the certified tier, semantic models on the enterprise platform, metric dictionary and review gate.
  • Partner spokes: two analysts serve Quality, one or two serve Regulatory and one serves Clinical. They join partner cadences and gather requirements directly.
  • Starting hypothesis: partner alignment is the likeliest inherited arrangement. Month one confirms or corrects it.
  • Platform boundary: IT owns the platform. The hub owns the model layer: discipline at the core, flexibility at the edge.

Two hats.

  1. Partner service: each analyst serves a partner and gathers its requirements.
  2. Domain stewardship: that analyst also owns one domain’s answer to which number is right, whichever partner asks. Domains include complaints, corrective actions, submissions and regulatory intelligence, and clinical data.

Stewardship and service use the same hours. Agree the trade during discovery (§1), then protect it through the intake rule.

Three endorsement levels

Certified, Promoted and none are BI platform quality labels, not a decision about regulatory status. For each output, agree three separate questions with Quality:

  1. What is its intended use?
  2. What assurance does the quality system require, scaled to process risk? The existing basis is ISO 13485 clause 4.1.6 and FDA’s February 2026 software-assurance guidance.
  3. Is it an electronic record under an applicable predicate rule?
  • Certified: validated for its intended use, the full accountability path, may be cited in a regulated record.
  • Promoted: reviewed by a named reviewer, restricted to uses Quality has agreed need no validation.
  • Uncertified: exploratory, labelled, never cited in a regulated record.

The accountability path below applies in full to the certified tier.

From requirements to acceptance

Every layer on the left (what we intend) has a partner on the right (how we check it), and the builder of a layer never verifies it.

Layer Who Owns
Accountable owner The process owner in Regulatory, Quality or Clinical Signs requirements in (what “right” means) and validation out (it does what we said)
Specification and system verification The manager Requirements written down; the whole checked against the owner’s intent; the queue’s rules; the structure
Independent review Two named reviewers: the senior analyst for metric logic and outputs, the architect for models and pipelines, each reviewing the other The check the builder cannot do on their own work; the technical review record
Build Analysts and engineers, working with AI Pipelines, models, tools, dashboards
Platform IT, with the architect Sources, environments, access, validated change control

The reviewers.

The proposed control

  • Name two qualified, independent reviewers in the procedure. Limit certification rights to them and the manager.
  • Keep the pull-request approval as the technical review record. Process-owner acceptance and any required Quality approval remain separate; the applicable quality procedure determines the signatures.

Capacity and development

  • Rotate peer review on other work for learning and coverage.
  • Have a junior shadow a reviewer in rotation to build the bench.
  • If no team member can fill a reviewer seat on day one, the manager holds it until the gap is filled and states that gap in the recommendation. The builder still cannot verify the same layer.

Basis for the proposal: adjacent practice uses qualified second-person review with a signed record, and software teams use required review from named owners on protected paths. That supports the arrangement; it does not present the proposed reviewer seats as an existing team practice.

The horizontal split, held loosely.

Whether pods by data domain fit better than spokes by partner is decided at day 90 from a tagged intake log. This is a hypothesis, and the log is the test.

Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Built a BI function of six hires and one placement from early-career people, each hired for the gap the team had at the time, with deliberately complementary skills; two engineers built the warehouse reviewing each other’s work with the manager as mediator.
  • Established a repeatable certification method at a global manufacturer: name the KPI, evaluate the source and its input controls, clean bronze to gold, verify the business logic field by field with the experts, certify and catalogue.
  • The tiers and the reviewer seats are proposed
How this would be checked
  • Every certified output carries an independent reviewer’s signature in the record and the approvals the procedure requires. The certifier group is two reviewers plus the manager, configured in the platform.
  • The steward list is published. Intended use, assurance and record status are recorded per output.
  • The structure recommendation at day 90 cites the intake log

3 · Cadence

Principle: mandatory work is never ranked against discretionary work.

One board, four classes of service.

  • Expedite: a feed serving a running regulatory clock is down. One at a time; if two arrive together, the manager sequences them and explains the decision.
  • Fixed date: regulatory reporting, management review packs, periodic surveillance reports, submissions and audits.
  • Standard: the backlog.
  • Intangible: the metric dictionary, catalogue, stewardship and technical debt.

Everything stays visible on one board. Mandatory work bypasses discretionary ranking, not the board. Publish a service-level expectation for each class. Reserved capacity for Expedite and Fixed date starts as an explicit assumption and is replaced by a quarter of data.

A two-week rhythm on top.

  • Every two weeks: planning, review and a retrospective. The manager’s own miss goes on the retrospective board first.
  • Weekly one-on-ones: ask what support is missing and where the manager’s blind spots are.
  • Decision timing: pin review meetings to the quality calendar, including monthly trending, quarterly management review and annual reports, rather than forcing every decision into a fortnight.

Coverage.

Every pipeline supporting a reportability or vigilance decision needs:

  • A named primary and secondary owner, plus a published calendar.
  • A manual fallback that protects the deadline and a defined escalation path.
  • An agreed owner outside the overlap window. Discovery determines whether the team or an existing service owner holds that responsibility.

Remote by design.

  • Written first: document the change before announcing it; use the handbook as the reference.
  • Shared rhythm: one weekly team call on a live document, one biweekly partner review and a written daily standup in the channel.
  • Access: record meetings; attendance is optional except for decision owners. State an overlap window across time zones.
  • Bridge the silos: each analyst meets the primary process owner every two weeks; the team shows its work to the division monthly.
Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Ran daily calls with development, weekly calls with site leads and biweekly calls with plant managers on a three-plant reconciliation; the cadence was set by who needed to decide what.
  • Led a global analytics team for four years without meeting it in person.
  • Opened every biweekly retrospective with my own miss so that the team’s misses became safe to name; late in that tenure, when something slipped, it was usually mine, because the team’s part of the process had become robust.
  • The classes of service are proposed
How this would be checked
  • WIP against limit, cycle time by class of service, and statutory clock misses attributable to data (target zero) on the scorecard.
  • The retro board carries the manager’s item every time.
  • One-on-ones held at cadence.
  • Expedite share of throughput under ten percent, or the platform is the problem

4 · Prioritization and saying no

Principle: say no with a rule, and say it with an alternative.

Seven fields for every request

One intake form captures seven fields:

  1. The question.
  2. The decision it changes.
  3. When the answer is needed and why, including a regulatory clause where relevant.
  4. The audience.
  5. What has already been tried.
  6. Whether the output will be a regulated record or cited in one.
  7. Which existing asset was checked first.

Respond within one business day: do now; schedule; self-serve on certified content; or decline with an alternative and a logged reason. Effort informs triage within the priority rule below.

The priority rule

  1. Statutory clock and patient safety.
  2. Audit and compliance commitments.
  3. Management review deliverables.
  4. Improvement.
  5. Exploratory work.

Clock-bound work is a separate class of service on the same board; it is not ranked against discretionary work.

Who decides the monthly ranking

Meet monthly: Regulatory, Quality and Clinical rank Standard-class requests with the full backlog in view. Join an existing divisional forum if one is already in place.

  • Triage analyst: drives the process.
  • Partners: contribute priorities and requirements.
  • Quality: retains a compliance veto.
  • Manager: is the one approver within the forum’s ranking and the sponsor’s delegated authority.
  • Sponsor: settles deadlocks or contested rules.

One name owns each decision.

Distributed intake.

The spokes gather requirements with their partners; planning is the integration point. The manager owns the rules of the queue, not the queue.

Install order.

  1. Week one: use the intake form to build the inventory.
  2. By day 30: establish the forum, with inherited promises visible.
  3. At day 60: add rotating triage if volume justifies it.
Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Intake on my first team started single-threaded through me. A high performer finished early, asked for more, and nothing was scoped because I was the bottleneck.
  • He said he could gather the content team’s requirements himself; I changed the intake process so he did, and folded it into sprint planning. On the three-plant reconciliation, two of six KPI disagreements would not settle in the room, so I took them up a rung with the trade-offs mapped and asked the plant leaders to render a verdict so we could move.
  • The form, the rule and the forum are proposed
How this would be checked
  • Requests answered within one business day.
  • Declines carry an alternative and a logged reason; the count is on the scorecard, not hidden.
  • Escalations resolved, with how long they took.
  • The inherited promises are all visible to the forum in month one

5 · Leading through influence

Principle: do you care, and can you help.

Most of this job is interpersonal, and none of the partners report to the manager.

Serve the decision-maker.

When definitions conflict, start with the decision: what is being measured, and what will change because of it? Prefer the more accurate and actionable definition, not the loudest voice.

If the disagreement remains, prepare one decision page:

  • The competing definitions.
  • Where each is strong and weak.
  • The tradeoffs the owner must choose between.

Do the work that makes the owner’s decision easier, then hand over a decision rather than an unresolved problem.

Tell each other the truth, with care.

Reconciliation cannot be done in the abstract. It means telling an expert that a number they have reported for years is not the one. Said plainly, said with the evidence, said once. The same message in a good month and a bad one.

Governance as change control.

Use the controlled-change structure the partners already know:

  1. Describe and justify the change.
  2. Record its impact and risk.
  3. Obtain approval from the affected function.
  4. Verify the change and check effectiveness at 90 days.

Name the people who already resolve disputed numbers as stewards in writing, with time recognized by their managers. Start with two volunteer process owners and one certified dataset that removes a real pain within 60 days; have them present it to peers.

Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Three plants on three continents reported the same twelve KPIs with different math.
  • Reconciled four definitions site by site, escalated two to the plant leaders with the trade-offs mapped, delivered one dashboard, cut the cycle from the 15th to the 5th and about 430 hours a year.
  • Worked with 23 manufacturing sites in eleven countries, with ongoing multi-project work at fourteen of them, and 200-plus stakeholders, without authority over any of them
How this would be checked
  • Forum attendance by function.
  • A trust item (“I trust the accuracy of our reporting”) two or three times a year.
  • Question-to-decision time by partner, judged by the decision maker.
  • Standards shipped as controlled changes with their 90-day effectiveness check closed

6 · Governance

Principle: a number is trusted when its owner, definition, source and reviewer are written down.

The metric dictionary.

Every certified measure records:

  • Its named business owner and definition.
  • Its source and version.
  • Its lineage from source to screen.

The dictionary is the first deliverable, maintained as a product rather than a one-time document.

The certification gate.

Before a measure becomes certified:

  1. Evaluate the source and its input controls.
  2. Clean the data along a stated path.
  3. Verify the business logic field by field with its business expert.
  4. Obtain the independent reviewer’s signature and the approvals required by the procedure.
  5. Publish the catalogue entry.

An intake request cannot confer certification. The measure enters the certified tier through this gate.

Scope, per output.

Decide with Quality, for each analytics product:

  • Its intended use and required assurance under the quality system.
  • Whether it is an electronic record under a predicate rule, or is relied on for a regulated activity.

Neither storage location nor endorsement label settles those questions. Validation is proportionate to process risk, before use and after change, with records. A tested, version-controlled workflow supplies validation evidence.

Reuse and retirement.

Reuse

  • Keep a small certified core of semantic models, with certification rights limited to the two named reviewers and manager and a written checklist.
  • Review reports per certified model and certified share of consumption quarterly.

Retirement by review

  1. Flag an asset below a stated view count over 90 days with no named owner.
  2. Check retention requirements, dependencies, legal holds and authorized disposition before moving it.
  3. Archive retired assets restorably. Never delete required records or the evidence needed to reproduce them.
Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Governed 200-plus critical data elements at a global manufacturer; the reports were built to the standards the quality system set and checked by internal audits.
  • The certification method above is the one I ran there.
  • On this site, source reconciliation and separate re-derivation are evidenced per module in the Build by Build inventory; one module that could not balance its reconciliation published the gap with its four reasons in order of size rather than tuning it away, and declined to certify two fields the publisher’s own codebook warned about.
  • Retirement by review and the dictionary as a product are proposed
How this would be checked
  • Share of regulated-decision outputs on certified data, rising from the day-30 baseline. Every certified measure has owner, definition, source, version, lineage, reviewer and record status in the catalogue.
  • First-pass verification rate with its balancing measures (§9). Reuse ratio quarterly.
  • Every retirement has a review record

7 · AI, used well

Principle: a person owns every regulated decision.

How I build with AI.

  • Specify: set the architecture, data model and measurement definitions in writing.
  • Build: AI coding agents implement the specification; the author reviews the output and validates it rather than hand-writing the code.
  • Verify: use the check and review methods recorded for the particular build. The Build by Build inventory shows which controls appeared when; methods and scope vary across the portfolio.

Claim Integrity includes a separate numerical re-derivation and a restricted simulated reading review. That review is not represented as certified blindness or independent evaluator custody. The controls developed over successive builds; their presence and limits are recorded rather than assumed.

Three steps before using AI

  1. State the requirement: treat the AI tool as software within the quality system and write down its intended use.
  2. Verify before reliance: compare its output with a known answer set and scale assurance to process risk. The existing reference is FDA’s February 2026 software-assurance guidance and its business-intelligence example: higher risk where data integrity is affected, and limited additional assurance beyond the vendor’s for reporting used only for monitoring.
  3. Set the boundary: a person owns every regulated decision. AI may order the queue, but cannot decide what enters it or delay a case past its clock.

Before processing complaint text or controlled documents, discovery must establish the approved environment, access and supplier arrangements. Those permissions are a prerequisite, not an assumption.

  • Protect the deadline: every case stays in controlled human review inside its clock. Ordering cannot delay a deadline; aging cases escalate by rule; a manual fallback exists.

Chasing edges.

The reason to use AI at all is the accumulation of small advantages: earlier information, more cycles, less manual work. “The way we’ve always done it” is not a reason. Permission to try is explicit when the intent is right and the control is in place.

The method behind these controls, from a requirement to an answer specified separately from the implementation to a check that can expose a mistake, is written up as a playbook with four experiments that run in the browser: Validating AI-Assisted Analytics.

Experience and proposed check
Experience and scope
  • Nine governed analytics modules built on this site in under four months, each to a written specification.
  • From the fourth: a frozen source with a stated as-of date, a validation report committed beside the data, chart review against the written standard, a reading panel, and a proof that the checks can fail.
  • One module runs a scheduled pipeline that re-asserts its invariants on every run (key uniqueness, no negative durations, no closed matter without an end date) and writes a per-run record naming each check and its outcome; it publishes its reconciliation whether or not it balances.

How this would be checked
  • Agreement rate against a reference set and predefined acceptance criteria before go-live.
  • For any triage assist in production: no case past its clock, monthly human-reviewed sample with its denominator, missed-reportable count, recall trend, revalidation events, every record showing the suggestion, the decision and the owner, reported to Quality leadership as a quality-system control

8 · People

Principle: coach to the person, and measure yourself by where they go.

Build the team from the people you have and the people you can afford, hire for the gap, point each person at where they want to go, coach them up, and measure yourself by where they end up.

Practice 01

Coach to the person; change the format before you change the person

Change the support format when asking for help is the barrier.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

An analyst with real skill missed requirements and ran long; telling her to ask for help did not take because asking was not natural to her, so I changed the retro so that help came to her: two items of hers on the board every time, opened to the team. She got faster and was still on the team when I left

How this would be checked

One-on-one cadence held; a development plan per person

Practice 02

Grow people into the next role by what they own, not by title first

Grow the role by changing what the person owns.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

An analyst taught himself the back end on his own time; I backed it, proposed a lead title, and changed what he owned: direct requirements with stakeholders without me in the middle

How this would be checked

Internal promotions; scope changes recorded

Practice 03

Hire for the gap, skills bar then fit, and interview the thinking

Assess the missing skill and the thinking behind the work.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

Six hires over four and a half years, each for the gap the team had at the time. After one hire cleared the skills bar and struggled on diligence, every interview gained a process question: what would you do if you hit this, walk me through your thinking

How this would be checked

Hiring notes carry the process question and its answer

Practice 04

Hire for difference

Complementary strengths can serve different partners effectively.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

A hire from banking, twenty years older than the team, SQL on her resume but Excel in practice (Power Query, VBA, complex accounting workbooks), put almost exclusively on the accounting partner. She got the work done her way, and I could tell her reports by their look before I read them; that is where my standard for how a report should look began

How this would be checked

Complementary strengths on the skill map, with backup coverage for every critical skill

Practice 05

Measure success by where people go

Development includes helping people move beyond the available role.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

My first hire outgrew the budget; I could not secure what he was worth and did not try to hold him. Of the six hires, four were in their first analytics role and a fifth in her first with the title. Where they went: a customer data platform architect, a senior data engineer at a large technology company, a senior BI analyst at a national retailer, an eight-year run as a BI analyst at a global information firm, a doctoral candidate in epidemiology, and a retirement after a decade in BI

How this would be checked

Alumni outcomes recorded; nobody held below their worth

Practice 06

Advocate for the right seat early

Discuss duties, support and fit early rather than letting uncertainty drift.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

One person came to the team by placement rather than through its process; the work was acceptable and the fit never settled. A stronger manager says in the first month: keep her where she is succeeding. I let it drift. The lesson is mine

How this would be checked

A conversation at 30 days with the person and the sending manager about duties, support and fit; a conversation, not a decision

Practice 07

Celebrate the person, unite on the standard

Celebrate different backgrounds while holding one shared work standard.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

A team from different backgrounds and countries; we ate each other’s food and learned each other’s stories, and held one rule: fast, accurate, high quality, and each other’s backs. No infighting in four and a half years

How this would be checked

Conflicts raised early and resolved; the escalation path used when needed, not avoided

Practice 08

Go first when something breaks

The manager makes the first miss safe to discuss.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

My own miss on the retro board first, every time

How this would be checked

The manager’s item on every retro board

Practice 09

Ask the team where your blind spots are, on a cadence

Ask for blind spots regularly and act on what changes the answer.

Experience and proposed check

Experience

Two standing questions every week. One answer changed the intake process

How this would be checked

Asked and logged every week

9 · How we’d know it’s working

Principle: the standard is stated first; the measures follow.

This scorecard is proposed; the pieces of it existed on teams I ran, the whole did not.

  • Rhythm: one page, quarterly, baselined at day 30.
  • Each line: a trend, target and action level, with a written definition and the decision it informs before activation.
  • Balance: include at least one perceptual measure. Activity counts alone are insufficient.

Two headlines.

  • For the team: first-pass verification rate, with rework after release, escaped defects and review aging alongside it.
  • For partners: question-to-decision time.

Both are proposed measures, baselined at day 30. The zero statutory-clock-miss figure is a target, not a reported result.

10 · What I don’t know yet

Principle: you don’t know all the answers. Find those who do.

What I would ask the sponsor before believing any of the above, in the order it matters:

  1. Who does each person report to today, and what stays with their former managers?
  2. What existing duties can stop or change to fund review, stewardship and coverage, and what authority and budget does this seat carry?
  3. Does one complaint-handling system serve every business unit, or several, and who owns the platform?
  4. Does Quality Systems or IT already run a validation function for quality-system software that the team’s reviewers should plug into?
  5. Does a divisional data governance forum already exist to join?
  6. Who owns after-hours decisions and the manual fallback when a statutory feed fails today?
  7. Which approved environment may process complaint text and controlled documents for AI, under what access and supplier arrangements?
  8. Has a reorganization already been announced to the team?
  9. Does the division have a quality metrics dashboard the scorecard should sit inside?
  10. Of the AI uses, which is first, how should its outputs be validated before anyone relies on them, and what should be different at six months and at twelve?

What would change the plan

  • Split complaint systems: the first deliverable becomes the dictionary alone.
  • Existing validation function: the reviewers become its members.
  • Announced reorganization: the day-30 hypothesis becomes the design.
  • Earlier structure answer needed: the day-60 operating arrangements are the answer; the day-90 document is the evidence.
  • No capacity for the additional responsibilities: reduce scope before reducing the standard.

A win for a person is a win for the whole team.
