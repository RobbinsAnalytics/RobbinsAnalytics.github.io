The title, defined

An AI Analytics Architect owns the what and the proof, not the how.

The job has three moves. None of them is writing the code.

  1. 01

    Business rules

    Specify

    Turn a business need into a precise spec: the data model, the measure definitions, the matching rules, and the invariants that must hold.

    Correct is defined before anything is built, so there is something to check the build against.

  2. 02

    Delivery

    Direct the build

    AI tools or an engineer do the building; either works. The job is to aim the build at the spec, not to pour the concrete.

    That leverage is why one person now covers ground that used to take a team.

  3. 03

    Evidence

    Verify

    Nothing ships on trust. Every number is re-derived a second, independent way and reconciled against source, and a check that disagrees stops the publish.

    Trust what you can test.

Shared foundation

See the system behind the requests

Separate requests often point back to the same underlying system. A sales report, a service dashboard and a finance review may all describe the same customers, products, locations and dates.

Define those shared building blocks once, including what each means and how records connect, and each report can use the same foundation.

The architectural work is recognizing that overlap, deciding what should be shared and where differences matter, then giving the build a structure that can be checked.

I do not hand-write the code, and that is a choice. There are plenty of people who write code well. Specifying and verifying AI-built analytics inside a business domain is the rare skill, and it is the one this site is built to demonstrate.

Selected outcomes

  • 01

    8factories on one reporting system

    Built the Open Quality Notification reporting that eight factories ran on. I built the original myself, before there was a development team, sourcing from SAP directly so nothing was copied between workbooks. It scaled from one site to 8, cut about 32 hours a week of manual work, brought average closure time down 5 days, and reduced the notifications aging past 30 days by 20%. Refresh went from weekly by hand to hourly during business hours, on a cadence set by how the work actually runs rather than by how often we could run it.

  • 02

    12KPIs, one definition each, three plants

    Gave three plants one set of numbers. The monthly operations report pulled from Murrysville, Böblingen and Shenzhen, each calculating the same 12 KPIs differently. I ran the initiative end to end, surfaced 6 definition misalignments and reconciled 4 of them myself, and delivered one automated Power BI report on aligned definitions. It removed about 36 hours of manual compilation a month and moved the reporting cycle from the 15th to the 5th.

  • 03

    200+Critical Data Elements governed

    Governed data at enterprise scale. I standardized 200+ Critical Data Elements and common data model definitions across a global manufacturing platform, enforcing semantic consistency across systems that had none.

  • 04

    100+hours a week of manual reporting removed

    Eliminated 100+ hours a week of manual reporting. I moved disconnected spreadsheets and point-in-time extracts into automated, governed dashboards.

  • 05

    $80Ka year out of the reporting estate

    Took $80K a year out of the reporting estate. I cataloged every pipeline under the Operations umbrella, traced each to the reports it fed, measured what people actually used, and retired the dead pipelines and over-frequent refreshes. No report was moved or deleted.

  • 06

    1BI function, founded and staffed

    Founded a BI function. I proposed the department at Lowe's/ATG Stores, hired its foundational team, and managed six analysts and developers through the build of the data warehouse, the KPI definitions and the stewardship model.
